US Visa Refused Under Section 214(b): What It Means and How to Reapply

A 214(b) refusal is the most common outcome for refused visitor and student visa applicants. It is not a ban, and it is not permanent — but reapplying without changing anything usually produces the same answer.

Written for applicants in Panvel, Taloja and Navi Mumbai · Checked against US State Department guidance on 18 November 2026

What Section 214(b) actually says

Under Section 214(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, every applicant for a nonimmigrant visa is presumed to be an intending immigrant until they satisfy the consular officer otherwise. The burden sits entirely with the applicant, not the officer.

In practice the officer is asking one question: have you shown that your economic, family and social ties outside the United States are strong enough that you will leave at the end of your authorised stay, and that what you plan to do there matches the visa you applied for?

If the answer is not clearly yes, the application is refused under 214(b). The State Department's own manual is explicit that this is not a permanent ineligibility — an applicant may qualify later by showing changed circumstances — and that no waiver exists for it.

What a 214(b) refusal is not. It is not a finding that you lied, not a penalty, and not a mark that follows you forever. The State Department's guidance states plainly that fitting a demographic profile — being young, or single, for example — is not by itself grounds for refusal. The decision must rest on your specific circumstances.

There is no appeal

The refusal letter says the decision cannot be appealed, and that is accurate. There is no review body and no form to contest it. A supervisor may overturn a 214(b) refusal only where they believe it was made in error, which is rare and not something an applicant can request as a process.

The route forward is a new application, not an appeal of the old one.

What reapplying requires

According to State Department guidance, reapplying means starting the application again:

  • A new application form and a new photograph
  • Paying the visa application fee again — the previous fee is a non-refundable processing fee and is not carried over
  • Booking a fresh interview appointment

There is no mandatory waiting period. You may reapply at any time. Consular officers are instructed neither to encourage nor discourage reapplication, so an officer saying "you can try again" is not a signal about your chances.

The part that actually decides the outcome

This is where most repeat refusals come from. State Department guidance is that an applicant reapplying should be prepared to present information that was not in the original application, or to show that their circumstances have changed.

A second interview with the same facts, the same documents and the same answers is, from the officer's perspective, the same application. Nothing has changed, so the assessment does not change either.

Meaningful change usually looks like one of these:

  • A genuine shift in employment, income, business ownership or property since the last application
  • Family circumstances that have materially changed
  • For students: a different or clearer academic plan, firmer funding, or a clearer link between the course and a career in India
  • Information you held but failed to convey — often the real problem, rather than an absence of ties

That last point matters more than people expect. Many refusals are not caused by weak ties but by ties that were never communicated clearly in a short interview, or by a DS-160 that contradicts what the applicant said aloud.

Before you book another interview

  1. Work out, honestly, which part of your profile did not convince the officer.
  2. Check that your DS-160 matches your real current circumstances and the account you will give in person.
  3. Decide whether something has genuinely changed. If nothing has, consider waiting until it has.
  4. Prepare to explain your plan and your reasons to return in your own words — briefly and consistently.

Interviews at Mumbai are short. Most applicants are not refused for lack of documents; they are refused because the picture the officer formed in two or three minutes did not answer the 214(b) question. Rehearsing a clear, truthful account of your plan matters as much as paperwork.
